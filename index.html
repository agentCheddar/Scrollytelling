Mirror

By Tyler Kunz

Mirror

Sylvia Plath

Is what you see in a mirror real? Well, not exactly, because you see a mirrored version of yourself. The popular symbol of a broken mirror is very far from the truth: it is a distorted version of reality that makes the person looking into it feel broken.

But what if it’s not broken? Sylvia Plath explores this question in her poem “Mirror.”

Lines 1–2

The poem opens with the speaker (the mirror) telling the reader a riddle about who they are.

Riddles are meant to be mystical and deceiving, which sets up how the mirror is not what it claims.

Lines 1, 4, 5, 11 & 13

The mirror tries to tell the reader that it is the absolute truth.

Lines 2 & 4

The mirror is starting to feel like the villain of the story. The word “swallow” has negative connotations: it takes something in and doesn’t give back. The mirror also needs to clarify that it isn’t cruel.

Line 3

She’s saying here that what humans see is misted by love and dislike.

Line 7

This detail seems out of place, being the only description of the world we get so far. But it tells us that this mirror is likely in a little girl’s room.

Lines 7–8

This contradicts the mirror’s claim that it is truthful and unmisted by love or dislike. It clearly loves the wall on the other side of the room, which is more evidence that the mirror is not what it says it is.

Line 9

The girl is still young, and even though she looks in the mirror, it hasn’t had an effect on her yet.

I think this girl grows up to become the woman in the second stanza.

Line 10

This stanza shifts from being about a mirror (a household object that is just a thin film of silver with nothing behind it) to a lake (which also reflects what is above it but also conceals what is below). In this case the lake represents how the woman is trying to conceal her bodily insecurities.

A lake’s reflection is also less perfect. It is distorted by ripples, which could represent outside feelings.

Line 11

You wouldn’t have to “search the reaches” of a lake to find the true reflection. It should be right in front of you. The woman is actually searching underneath the lake for what she fears she’ll become, not what she actually is.

Line 12

Plath uses candles and the moon because they are soft sources of light, which makes people look younger.

This is a clear example of how the mirror is not neutral. It is calling these other ways to see yourself “liars.”

Line 14

This makes the lake seem evil, because it is “rewarded” with the agony of the woman.

Line 15

The woman is controlled by her anxieties (which she tries to find underneath the lake), and she keeps coming back to them.

This also kind of sounds like the lake is manipulating the woman, drawing her in.

Lines 9 & 16

This is in contrast to the last line of the previous stanza. The lake looks forward to seeing the woman, while the mirror just wants to see the wall.

Lines 17–18

This means that the woman cares too much about her appearance, looking at herself in the lake every day. Maybe if she didn’t look in the lake, the old woman wouldn’t come out.

Line 17

The lake didn’t drown the girl; the woman looking at the lake did.

Line 18

A very clear simile that shows that the mirror makes the woman think her older self will be ugly, like a terrible fish.

Thesis

While the mirror may claim to be the absolute truth and neutral, it is not. It prompts the woman into a cycle of self-examination which makes her anxieties about beauty and aging worse.

What’s not clear is if it was the woman who is to blame, or the mirror, or both.
